## 1. Summary

1. **What it is.** Investment banking (IB) is advisory and capital-raising work for companies, funds and governments: mainly selling or buying companies (M&A), raising equity (ECM) and debt (DCM, leveraged finance), and advising on restructurings. Juniors build financial models, valuations and presentations for senior bankers (Rothschild's own description of a Madrid first-year analyst job [4|V]).
2. **Who it suits.** People who like numbers and writing under time pressure, accept very long hours at the start, and want steep learning and a strong exit option (private equity, corporate development, other finance). It does not suit those who want predictable hours (see section 5).
3. **Spain is mostly M&A, mid-market and Big 4.** By number of deals advised in Spain in 2025 the leader in several league tables is not a bank but a Big 4 deal-advisory team (PwC first in LSEG, Mergermarket and Bloomberg rankings, as reported) [18|R]. Independent Spanish boutiques (Alantra) rank in the top five by count [18|R]. So "banca de inversión en España" is a mix: Spanish bank CIB divisions, international banks' Madrid teams, boutiques, and Big 4.
4. **Competitive.** No firm publishes acceptance rates for Spain. Firms ask for strong academic records (Citi's Madrid listing reportedly asks Spanish graduates for a grade of 7 or above [21|R]) and fluent English and Spanish [4|V]. Intake per firm in Madrid looks small (a Houlihan Lokey 2025 profile puts its whole Madrid office at 25 people [20|R]).
5. **Entry routes (three real ones).** (a) Madrid on the **off-cycle / placement / analyst programmes** of banks and boutiques, often as a 6–12-month internship that converts to a job [3|V][5|V][21|R]; (b) the **Santander CIB / BBVA CIB / CaixaBank CIB graduate programmes** [1|V][25|R][26|R]; (c) the **London route**: spring week (first/second year) → summer internship → graduate analyst offer, with a right-to-work question for Spanish citizens since Brexit [9|V][10|V].
6. **Pay (Spain).** Michael Page 2026 guide: analyst base €45–50K (national bank/boutique M&A) or €50–65K (international bank M&A), bonus 10–30% and 20–40% respectively [7|V]. London pay is much higher but sources disagree widely (section 6).
7. **Hours.** Famously long. JPMorgan introduced an 80-hour weekly cap for juniors in 2024 with exemptions for live deals [11|R]; a 2021 Goldman analyst survey (13 people) averaged ~95 hours [12|R].
8. **Calendar.** Madrid programmes run all year (off-cycle), Santander CIB's 2026–27 call opened in Aug–Sep [2|V]; London spring weeks open around Sep–Oct and fill on a rolling basis [27|R].
9. **Cycle risk.** Deal counts in Spain fell in 2025 while values rose (providers differ on the size) [16|R][19|R], so hiring in Madrid is cyclical.
10. **Proposed pages.** One hub plus: (i) Spanish CIB and Big 4 deal advisory, (ii) international banks and boutiques in Madrid, (iii) the London route (spring week to analyst), (iv) a "month by month" calendar page. Detail in section 2.

### Divisions inside an IB (what "M&A analyst" vs others means)

- **M&A / advisory**: sells/buys companies; most visible entry role. Rothschild's Madrid analysts rotate across M&A, "Debt and Restructuring" and equity advisory [4|V].
- **ECM / DCM / leveraged finance**: raising equity or debt. Santander CIB groups it under "capital markets, M&A, structured finance, trade finance, treasury management" [1|V].
- **Restructuring**: advising distressed companies. Houlihan Lokey's Madrid office hires in "Financial Restructuring" [20|R].
- **Coverage vs product**: coverage bankers own client relationships by sector or country; product bankers (M&A, ECM, DCM) execute. GS's Madrid ad is "Spain Country Coverage" analyst for experienced hires [35|R].

## 3. How the business works

**Clients.** Large and mid-sized companies, private-equity funds, family businesses, governments and state-owned entities. Clients pay for advice on a transaction or for raising money.

**Revenue model.**

- _Advisory (M&A):_ retainer plus a **success fee**, typically a percentage of deal value paid only when the deal closes [U: standard industry practice; no Spanish source collected]. This is why bankers' pay is volatile: when deals stop, bonuses fall.
- _Capital markets (ECM/DCM):_ underwriting and placement fees on the amount raised [U].
- _Banks with balance sheets_ also earn lending and trading income; Spanish CIB divisions combine these with advisory [1|V].

**Deal lifecycle (sell-side M&A, simplified)** [U, standard process; each step is the junior's workload]:

1. _Pitch:_ a bank competes to win the mandate; juniors build a pitch book (industry research, comparable companies and transactions, preliminary valuation). Rothschild lists "contributing content to pitch books" and industry research as analyst tasks [4|V].
2. _Preparation:_ financial model, teaser and information memorandum, buyer list.
3. _Marketing:_ outreach to buyers, NDAs, management presentations, first-round offers.
4. _Negotiation and due diligence_: data room, Q&A, second-round bids, legal documents (the lawyers are Big Law, see that dossier).
5. _Signing and closing._ Analysts coordinate the process "internally and by liaising with clients, their counterparties and other advisers" [4|V].

**Valuation toolkit juniors use:** discounted cash flow, trading comparables, transaction comparables, LBO-based valuation [4|V].

**Economics of a team.** A deal team is a pyramid (analyst, associate, VP, director/MD) [7|V uses these four levels]. Revenue comes from a small number of large fees per year; juniors are the cheapest labour on the pyramid, hence long hours. (Why: standard explanation, [U].)

**Spanish market context.**

- 2025 deal volume in Spain, by provider: LSEG €74.6bn across 1,885 announced deals; TTR through November €86.1bn across 2,823 deals (counts differ by definition); EY describes a ~15% fall in deal count and ~10% fall in value; Chambers cites ~€103bn for 2025 [16|R][18|R][19|R][17|R]. These sources **disagree**; the common message is "fewer, larger deals".
- Foreign vs domestic: private-equity-backed deals are a major source of M&A mandates (see the private equity dossier).

## 5. The real job

**Typical analyst tasks (verified list).** Research the industries clients operate in and support marketing with pitch-book content; build and maintain financial models; use valuation techniques (DCF, trading and transaction comps, LBO); coordinate the transaction process; start client contact [4|V].

**A typical week at entry level** (synthesis, [U]; the verified tasks above in practice):

- Mon–Tue: update models and comps for a live sale process; format a management presentation.
- Wed–Thu: turn comments from an associate/VP on a pitch book, often late at night; data-room Q&A on a live deal.
- Fri: circulate drafts, tidy files before the weekend; weekends depend on live deals.
- Almost every day: Excel, PowerPoint (formatting is a large part of the job), data sources such as Bloomberg/FactSet/Capital IQ (tools used by the industry; not verified for each Madrid firm [U]).

**Hours.**

- JPMorgan introduced an 80-hour weekly cap for junior bankers in September 2024, "in most cases", with live deals exempt [11|R].
- A 2021 internal Goldman Sachs survey of 13 first-year analysts reported ~95 hours a week on average and ~5 hours of sleep; the sample is tiny and the 80-hour limit was the analysts' proposal [12|R]. The bank raised junior pay about 30% afterwards [12|R].
- No Spain-specific survey was found. Writers should say that long hours are widely reported, not give a single number as the Spanish norm.

**Travel.** Mostly Madrid and London; cross-border deals can mean travel [U].

**Culture.** Hierarchical, deadline-driven, feedback is blunt [U].

**Myths to correct (with the evidence in this dossier):**

- _"Banca de inversión = Goldman and JPMorgan in Madrid."_ The Madrid presence of those firms is small and the programmes found are mostly off-cycle internships [3|V][21|R][22|R]; Spanish CIBs, boutiques and Big 4 do much of the volume [18|R].
- _"You need to be in London."_ Madrid has real programmes [3|V][4|V][5|V], but London remains the hub for the biggest firms [U].
- _"Only finance degrees get in."_ No source says that. Rothschild asks for a strong academic background, analytical and numerical skills and English plus Spanish [4|V]; previous corporate-finance experience is "taken into consideration" (not required) [4|V].
- _"Hours are the same everywhere."_ They vary by team and by whether a deal is live [11|R].

## 9. Selection process

No Spanish firm publishes its test content. What follows combines the programme pages and candidate/third-party reports; treat **the stage list as a pattern**.

**Typical stages [R/U, pattern from a recruiter/training article and candidate testimony, not a Spanish primary source]:**

1. Online application (CV in English; some firms also a cover letter; Morgan Stanley's Madrid listings ask only a CV and cover letter in English [24|R]).
2. Online tests: numerical/verbal reasoning or a cognitive test (~15–20 minutes) and situational judgement [32|R].
3. Video interview (HireVue-style): 30 seconds to prepare, ~1 minute to answer, one take, mostly behavioural and motivation questions (Goldman and Investec candidate descriptions) [32|R][29|U].
4. Superday / final round: several back-to-back interviews, behavioural plus technical, mix of junior and senior bankers [32|R].
5. One candidate reported for Santander (Madrid, March 2025): a mostly behavioural first round, a weekend case study, then a superday where the case is presented and two interviews follow (single testimony, Glassdoor) [29|U].

**Things verified on firm pages:**

- Goldman: you may apply to at most 4 business/location combinations per year; extra applications are auto-withdrawn [3|V].
- Rothschild lists the competencies: strong academics, English and Spanish, analytical and numerical skills, communication and teamwork, attention to detail, multitasking [4|V].
- Citi's Madrid placement analyst (reported): 2:1 equivalent, and Spanish university graduates need a grade of 7 or above [21|R].
- Process length: no Spanish-specific figure; the only figure I found (IE University's Finance sector report, marked "Confidential" on every page, so not citable as a primary source) says 4–8 weeks from application to offer for structured IB processes [34|U]. Do not publish without confirmation.

**What interviewers assess (synthesis [U]):** motivation for the firm and for M&A specifically; awareness of current deals; accounting basics and valuation (DCF, multiples) and how a deal affects the three statements (the tasks in [4|V] define what must be known); Excel; English; resilience.

**Common mistakes (synthesis [U]):** generic motivation ("finance is prestigious"); not following market news; not being able to walk through a DCF or explain why EV multiples differ; applying late (rolling processes, [27|R]); sending 5+ applications to the same firm (cap at 4 for GS [3|V]).

**Pass signals:** structured answers, specific recent deals, a clear reason for Spain vs London, evidence of Excel/financial modelling practice.

## 10. Preparation plan

Requirements (from sources): strong academic record; fluent English and Spanish [4|V]; for Citi Madrid, a grade of 7+ if graduating from a Spanish university [21|R]; no specific degree is required in the firm pages I read [4|V]. For the London route, right to work (section 8 and open questions). No licence is required to _apply_ [U: not verified; some UK roles need FCA-regulated examinations after joining].

**Plan by starting point (not by year of degree).**

_A. No finance background (start 20–24 weeks before the application you want):_

- Weeks 1–4: accounting basics (three statements), read the financial press daily (Expansión, Cinco Días, FT) [resources in section 11].
- Weeks 5–10: valuation (DCF, comps, precedent transactions), a free or paid course (Damodaran's free valuation course; Wall Street Prep/BIWS/CFI as paid options).
- Weeks 11–16: Excel and a first full model (3-statement + DCF); read the "behavioural" side: stories for teamwork, leadership, failure.
- Weeks 17–20: LBO basics, mock interviews, CV and English video practice.
- Weeks 21–24: apply early (rolling processes fill; section 8), attend firm events.

_B. Some finance (a finance degree or corporate finance course) (8–12 weeks):_ a refresh of accounting + DCF, 2–3 full models, daily press, mock technicals, firm research.

_C. Already have a finance internship (4–6 weeks):_ technical interview drills, deal discussion, networking for specific teams.

**How to practise.** Build a model for a listed Spanish company and write a one-page recommendation; keep a "deal diary" of 10 recent Spanish M&A deals; timed technical interviews with a friend; English answers recorded on video (timed 30 seconds prep/60 seconds answer as in [32|R]).

**How to show it on a CV.** One page in English; academic results (grade); finance internships, clubs (IE Finance Club, Esade Student Finance Society, Navarra Investment/Finance Society [30|V]); stock-pitch or CFA Research Challenge participation (Spain teams from CUNEF, ESADE, ICADE, IE and UPF were reported in a recent edition [30|R]); modelling projects; language certificates.

**Realistic expectations.** It is normal to apply to many firms and receive few interviews; most Spanish processes are rolling [27|R]. Offers from London require a right-to-work plan (open question 1).

## 13. Glossary (35 terms)

Definitions are standard textbook meanings (not market claims).

1. **M&A (mergers & acquisitions):** buying, selling or combining companies.
2. **ECM / DCM:** equity / debt capital markets; raising money by issuing shares or bonds.
3. **Leveraged finance:** debt financing for companies with high debt levels, often private-equity-owned.
4. **Sell-side / buy-side:** advising the seller / the buyer (also "buy-side" = investors such as PE funds).
5. **Mandate:** a formal engagement to advise or arrange a deal.
6. **Pitch (book):** the presentation that wins a mandate.
7. **Teaser:** a short anonymous summary of a company for sale.
8. **CIM / information memorandum:** detailed document describing the business for buyers.
9. **Data room:** secure repository for due diligence documents.
10. **Due diligence:** a buyer's verification of a target (financial, legal, tax, commercial).
11. **EBITDA:** earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortisation; a profit proxy.
12. **Enterprise value (EV):** value of the operating business = equity value + net debt (+ minorities etc.).
13. **Equity value:** value of the shareholders' stake.
14. **Net debt:** financial debt minus cash.
15. **EV/EBITDA:** a common valuation multiple.
16. **DCF (discounted cash flow):** valuing a company by discounting future cash flows.
17. **WACC:** weighted average cost of capital; the typical DCF discount rate.
18. **Comps (trading comparables):** valuing a company using multiples of similar listed companies.
19. **Precedent transactions:** valuing using multiples paid in similar past deals.
20. **LBO (leveraged buyout):** acquisition mainly financed with debt, repaid from the target's cash flows.
21. **IRR / MOIC:** annual return / money multiple achieved by an investor.
22. **Accretion / dilution:** whether a deal raises or lowers the acquirer's earnings per share.
23. **Synergies:** cost or revenue benefits from combining two companies.
24. **Tender offer / OPA (oferta pública de adquisición):** public offer to buy shares of a listed company, regulated in Spain by the CNMV.
25. **IPO / OPV:** initial public offering / oferta pública de venta.
26. **Bookbuilding:** collecting investor orders to set the issue price.
27. **Fairness opinion:** an adviser's opinion that a deal's price is fair.
28. **Bulge bracket:** the largest global investment banks.
29. **Boutique:** a smaller, advisory-only firm.
30. **Off-cycle:** an internship or hiring window outside the main (summer/graduate) calendar.
31. **Spring week:** short first-year insight programme (London).
32. **Superday:** a block of final interviews on one day.
33. **Placement analyst:** a long (often 6–12-month) analyst internship; Citi's Madrid role uses this name [21|R].
34. **Chinese wall:** information barrier between teams to prevent insider-trading conflicts.
35. **Pitch book vs deck:** "pitch book" is the sales presentation; "deck" any PowerPoint.
